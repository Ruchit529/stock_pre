# Stock Analysis Framework

## 1. Business analysis
	1. Overview
	2. Business marketcap
	3. Peer comparision
	4. Business type:
		1. product based
		2. service based
		3. asset based

## 2. Sector Analysis
	1. Sector
	2. Cycle (Cyclical / Defensive / Growth)
		1. Cyclical: performs well when economy is good, falls hard when economy is bad
			- Examples: Automobiles, Real Estate, Construction, Metals, Mining, Capital Goods, Hotels/Travel
			- High risk, high return potential; hardest to time; beginners should avoid
			- Strategy: Buy at economic upturn, hold (can't hold forever), sell when growth is peaking
		2. Defensive: stable growth in good times, still stable in bad times (essential-needs products)
			- Examples: FMCG, Pharma, Utilities, Telecom, Power
			- Low risk, moderate return; recession-proof; good for beginners
			- Strategy: buy anytime if fundamentals are strong, hold long term, sell rarely (only if fundamentals deteriorate)
		3. Growth: rapidly expanding sector, driven by adoption trend rather than the broader economy
			- Examples: IT, Renewable Energy, EVs, E-commerce, Fintech
			- Medium-high risk, highest return potential (multi-baggers); long runway but high valuations and intense competition
			- Strategy: buy early stage, hold very long term (10-20 yrs), sell on saturation; beginners should watch for at least 6 months before entering
	3. Ideal sector allocation by market condition
		1. Bull market: Cyclical 40% / Growth 30% / Defensive 30%
		2. Bear market: Defensive 60% / Growth 30% / Cyclical 10%
		3. Normal market: Cyclical 30% / Growth 30% / Defensive 40%
		4. As a beginner, default to assuming it's a bear market
	4. Tailwinds
	5. Risks
	6. Common sector-analysis mistakes to avoid
		1. Ignoring debt levels (high growth + high debt = danger; slow growth + low debt = safe)
		2. Comparing companies across different industries (different industries have different valuation norms)
		3. Falling for "cheap" stocks based on share price alone
		4. Quarterly-result obsession (quarterly fluctuation = noise, annual performance = signal)
		5. Analysis paralysis / half-baked learning
		6. Herd mentality / FOMO

## 3. Fundamental Analysis — Stage 1: Quick Screening (Gatekeeping)
Score the stock out of 10 points (9 points for Banks/NBFCs — see note below).

	1. Sales growth:
		1. if sales growth 1yr/5yr positive                    +0.5
		2. if sales growth 1yr/5yr negative                 red flag
		3. if sales growth 1yr  > 5yr                              +0.5
		4. benchmark:
			1. >10% - Excellent
			2. 5-10% - Good
			3. 0-5% - Slow Growth
			4. -ve%  -  Red Flag

	2. Profit Growth:
		1. if profit growth 1yr/5yr positive                   +0.5
		2.  if profit growth 1yr/5yr negative                  -0.5
		3. if profit growth 1yr  > 5yr                         +0.5
		4. benchmark:
			1. >15% -    excellent
			2. 10-15% - Good
			3. 5- 10% -  Acceptable
			4. 0-5%    -  slow
			5. -ve%    - Red Flag

	3. Operating Profit Margin OPM:
		1.  if OPM growth 1yr/5yr is above limit of benchmarks   +0.5
		2.  if OPM growth 1yr/5yr negative                        -0.5
		3. if OPM growth 1yr  > 5yr                               +0.5
		4. Benchmarks
				Industry               OPM
			1. IT SERVICES          22-28%
			2. Pharma                 25-35%
			3. FMCG                    18-25%
			4. Paints wires etc     18-28%
			5. Automobiles          8-12%
			6. Retail                      5-8%
			7. Steel metals           8-15%

	4. Return on Equity (ROE) — how much return is generated on shareholders' money
		1. if ROE > 20%                                                          +0.5
		2. if ROE is 10-20%                                                    +0.25
		3. if ROE of 5yr > ROE of 1yr                                       +0.5
		4. Buffett rule: look for a consistent 15%+ ROE over 10 years
		5. Benchmarks:
			1. >25% - Exceptional
			2. 20-25% - Excellent
			3. 15-20% - Good
			4. 10-15% - Acceptable
			5. <10% - Weak / Red Flag

	5. Return on Capital Employed (ROCE)
		1. if ROCE > 15%                                                    +0.5
		2. if ROCE is 10-15%                                                +0.25
		3. if ROCE of 5yr > ROCE of 1yr                                     +0.25
		4. if ROCE > ROE                                                    +0.25
		 (means debt is being used productively)
		5. Benchmarks:
			1. >20% - Exceptional
			2. 15-20% - Good
			3. 10-15% - Acceptable
			4. <10% - Weak

	6. Debt to Equity Ratio (D/E) — **not counted for Banks/NBFCs (see note below)**
		1. if D/E < 0.5                                                     +1
		2. if D/E is 0.5-1.0 (Good/Acceptable)                            +0.5
		3. Benchmarks:
			1. <0.5 - Excellent
			2. 0.5-1.0 - Good
			3. 1.0-1.25 - Acceptable
			4. >1.25 - Danger

	7. Current Ratio (CR) — liquidity check
		1. if CR > 2                                                                +1
		2. if CR is 1.5-2                                                          +0.5
		3. Benchmarks:
			1. >2 - Excellent
			2. 1.5-2.0 - Good
			3. 1-1.5 - Acceptable
			4. <1 - Danger

	8. Interest Coverage Ratio (IC)
		1. if IC > 10                                                               +1
		2. if IC is 5-10                                                           +0.5
		3. if IC is 3-5                                                             +0.25
		4. Benchmarks:
			1. >10 - Excellent / Ultra safe
			2. 5-10 - Good / Safe
			3. 3-5 - Acceptable
			4. <3 - Danger

	9. Price to Earnings Ratio (P/E) — valuation check
		1. Compare company P/E vs Industry P/E → x = Company P/E ÷ Industry P/E
		2. if x is 0.8-2                                                            +1
		3. if x is 2-2.5                                                            +0.5
		4. if x is 2.5-3.0                                                        +0.25
		5. if x > 3                                                                  Danger
		6. Benchmarks (typical minimum industry P/E):
			1. IT - 20
			2. FMCG - 30
			3. Pharma - 25
			4. Paints - 30
			5. Automobile - 15
			6. Banks - 15
			7. Commodities - 8
		7. Note: fundamentally strong companies can be overvalued; fundamentally weak companies can be undervalued — P/E alone is not the full picture

	10. Return on Assets (ROA)
		1. if ROA > benchmark minimum                                    +0.5
		2. if ROA of 1yr > average of 5yr                                  +0.5
		3. Benchmarks (minimum ROA):
			1. IT - 20%
			2. FMCG - 15%
			3. Manufacturing - 8-9%
			4. Capital Intensive - 5%
			5. Banks - 0.8%

	11. Final Scorecard (Stage 1)
		- Sales Growth | Profit Growth | OPM | ROE | ROCE | D/E | Current Ratio | Interest Coverage | P/E | ROA
		- **Banks/NBFCs: drop D/E from the table and score out of 9 instead of 10**

	12. Final Verdict
		| Score | Result |
		|---|---|
		| 8.5 and above | Exceptional |
		| 7.5 - 8.5 | Excellent |
		| 6.5 - 7.5 | Good |
		| Less than 6.5 | Avoid |
		- Proceed to Stage 2 (Deep Fundamental Analysis) only if Score ≥ 6.5

## 4. Deep Fundamental Analysis — Stage 2 (Deep Trend Analysis)
Analyze the trend of the past (typically past 2 years quarterly, 5 years annually).

	1. Quarterly Results trend — 2 points
		1. Sales YoY: consistently increasing (+1) / increasing with fluctuation (+0.75) / flat (0) / declining (red flag)
		2. OPM YoY: consistently increasing (+1) / constant or mostly increasing (+0.75) / constant (+0.5) / mostly constant with a few dips (+0.25) / consistently reducing (red flag)

	2. Profit & Loss statement trend — 3 points
		1. Sales (5yr): consistently increasing (+1) / increasing but flat at times (+0.75) / increasing with a dip (+0.5) / almost constant (+0.25) / declining (red flag)
		2. OPM (5yr): same scoring pattern as Sales above
		3. Net Profit: NP increasing & margin increasing (+1) / NP increasing & margin constant (+0.75) / NP increasing & margin fluctuating (+0.5) / NP increasing but margin decreasing (+0.25) / NP decreasing (red flag)

	3. Balance Sheet trend — 1 point
		- Reserves vs Borrowings trend: Reserves growing while borrowings shrink/stay flat scores highest (+1), progressively lower for weaker combinations (+0.75 / +0.5 / +0.25); red flag in all other cases

	4. Cash Flow trend — 1 point
		- Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) vs Net Profit: CFO increasing with margin increasing (+1) down to CFO increasing but margin decreasing (+0.25); NP decreasing is a red flag
		- Debtor Days: should never exceed 120 days; consistently decreasing (+1) / almost constant (+0.75) / fluctuating (+0.5) / increasing within 50% (+0.25) / increased more than 50% in the past 5 years (red flag)

	5. Ratios trend (ROE/ROCE) — 2 points
		- ROCE consistently increasing (+1) / maintained (+0.75) / increasing but fluctuating (+0.5) / decreasing (red flag)

	6. Shareholding Pattern trend — 1 point
		- Track Promoter / Public / FII / DII holding trend over time
		- Red flags: promoter holding decreasing, FII holding decreasing, DII holding decreasing
		- Public holding should never exceed 50%; ideal split example: Promoter 51% / Public 49% / FII+DII the rest

	7. Business Model & Competitive Advantage (Qualitative / MOAT analysis)
		1. Key questions:
			1. How does the company create value? (product/service, customer, problem solved)
			2. How does the company capture value? (pricing model, revenue streams)
			3. Is it scalable? (can it grow without proportional cost increase, asset-light vs asset-heavy)
		2. Business model types: Subscription, Marketplace, Manufacturing (branded vs commodity), Asset-light vs Asset-heavy, Freemium
		3. 5 types of economic MOAT:
			1. Brand Moat (e.g. Asian Paints, Nestle, Apple, Titan)
			2. Network Effect Moat (e.g. Zerodha, WhatsApp, VISA, Mastercard, LinkedIn)
			3. Cost Advantage Moat — from scale, location, technology, or supply chain (e.g. Reliance, DMart, Maruti Suzuki)
			4. Switching Cost Moat (e.g. Banking/Enterprise software, Accounting software, Insurance)
			5. Regulatory/Patent Moat (e.g. Pharma patents, spectrum licenses, defense contracts, liquor licenses)
		4. How to identify a MOAT — 5 tests:
			1. Can competitors easily copy the business?
			2. Will this advantage last 10+ years?
			3. Has ROE been consistently high for 10+ years?
			4. Can the company raise prices without losing customers?
			5. Is market share stable or growing over 5 years?

## 5. Technical Analysis & Valuation
	1. Valuation — is the entry price reasonable?
		- Good company + expensive price → loss or long wait
		- Good company + fair price → good returns
		- Good company + cheap price → excellent returns
		1. P/E Ratio Valuation (primary method)
			- Compare company P/E vs Industry P/E; if company P/E > 2x industry P/E, generally avoid
			- Fair Price = Industry P/E × Company EPS; compare to current stock price to judge over/undervaluation
		2. PEG Ratio (P/E to Growth — for growth stocks)
			- PEG = P/E Ratio ÷ Earnings Growth Rate
			- <1: Undervalued | 1-1.5: Fairly valued | 1.5-2.0: Getting expensive | >2: Overvalued
			- Best suited for IT, Pharma, Consumer discretionary; not suitable for Banks, mature companies, or low-growth cyclicals
		3. Price to Book Ratio (P/B — for asset-heavy businesses like Banks, Real Estate, Manufacturing, Insurance)
			- P/B = Market Price per Share ÷ Book Value per Share
			- Banks: 1.0-1.5 Very cheap | 1.5-2.0 Fair | 2-3 Reasonable | >3 Expensive
			- Manufacturing: <1.5 Cheap | 1.5-3.0 Fair | >3.0 Expensive
		4. Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) — advanced/brief intro
			- Company value = present value of future cash flows
		5. Margin of Safety (Warren Buffett's golden rule)
			- Margin of Safety = (Fair Value − Current Price) ÷ Fair Value
			- Only invest when price is at least 20% below fair value
			- Guards against wrong EPS estimates, wrong industry P/E assumptions, or lower-than-expected future growth

	3. Entry & Exit Strategies
		1. Entry methods:
			1. Lump Sum — full capital deployed at once; best for a market crash, a deeply undervalued stock, a windfall, or an experienced investor; high timing risk
			2. SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) — no timing needed, removes emotion, averages volatility; best for salaried/regular income, beginners, long-term (10+ yr) goals; slower wealth creation
			3. Tranched Buying — hybrid of lump sum + SIP combined
		2. Exit scenarios (any of these should trigger a review):
			1. Fundamentals deteriorating (structural problem, not temporary)
			2. Stock becomes overvalued
			3. Better opportunity available elsewhere
			4. Portfolio rebalancing need
			5. Investment goal achieved
			6. Investment mistake realized
			7. Personal emergency (need for funds)
		3. Fundamental deterioration red flags to watch for exit: management issues, outdated business model, market share loss to competitors, declining ROE/ROCE, rising debt, shrinking profit margins

	4. Portfolio Construction
		1. How many stocks:
			1. Concentrated (Buffett style) — max 5 stocks; high focus, high returns if right; not very practical for the Indian market
			2. Focused — 6-10 stocks; beginner friendly, decent return potential
			3. Balanced (recommended) — up to 25 stocks; e.g. 17 large-cap / 5 mid-cap / 3 small-cap
		2. Sector Diversification
			- Indian sectors to diversify across: IT Services, Banking, FMCG, Auto, Pharma/Healthcare, Infrastructure, Oil & Gas, Metals, Chemicals, Consumer Discretionary, Capital Goods, Telecom
			- Rule: no single sector > 20% of portfolio; hold a minimum of 4-5 sectors, up to 8; avoid highly correlated sectors
		3. Position Sizing
			1. Equal weightage — simple, no confusion
			2. Conviction-based weightage (using the fundamental scorecard): score ≥16 → 5% weight; 14-16 → 2.5%; <14 → 1.25%
		4. Core-Satellite Strategy — Core (70-80%, stable/safe) + Satellite (20-30%, growth/high-conviction)
		5. Portfolio Rebalancing — trigger points: after govt rules/regulations change, after quarterly results, after a stock reaches its target
		6. Common mistakes: over-diversification, blind equal weighting, home bias, incorrect sector timing, ignoring valuations, herd/copy-trading mentality, ignoring risk management, recency bias, emotional attachment, ignoring the tax impact of frequent trades

	5. Psychology & Risk Management
		1. Key emotions to manage: fear, greed, FOMO, panic, overconfidence, regret
		2. Risk management basics:
			1. Emergency fund — 6-12 months of expenses, non-negotiable, kept separate from equity
			2. Age-based debt/equity allocation — e.g. Age 25 → 15% debt/85% equity; Age 35 → 25/75; Age 45 → 35/65; Age 55 → 50/50
			3. Debt instruments for the safe portion: FD, PPF, NSC, Post Office Senior Citizens Scheme, Debt Mutual Funds, AA/AAA Corporate Bonds, Govt Bonds
			4. No over-concentration in position sizing
			5. Use a mental or technical stop-loss
			6. Don't act on news headlines alone
		3. Investor psychology traps:
			1. FOMO — apply the "48-hour rule": wait 2 days and let the excitement settle before acting
			2. Panic selling at market bottoms — driven by loss aversion (losses hurt roughly 2x more than equivalent gains feel good)
